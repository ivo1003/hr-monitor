After Stop the app measures heart rate recovery for 60 s – stop right after a hard effort and stand or sit still. DFA α1 needs ~2 min of clean RR data; α1 ≈ 0.75 marks your aerobic threshold, ≈ 0.5 the anaerobic threshold. Breathing rate is only reliable at rest or easy effort.

Live (last 5 min)

Morning readiness (HRV) test

Do it every morning right after waking up – before coffee, after the toilet if needed. Lie on your back, relax, breathe normally and don't move or talk. The first 30 s are used to settle and are not counted.

Orthostatic test (lie → stand)

Lie still for 2 minutes. When you hear the beep, stand up calmly and stand still for another 2 minutes. A larger rise in heart rate than usual, or slower settling, can be a sign of fatigue or overreaching. Best done in the morning, a few times a week.

Dots = daily values, line = 7-day average, shaded = your normal range (30-day mean ± 1 SD). A 7-day average sliding below the band = accumulated fatigue/stress.

Resting heart rate – 90 days

From morning tests. A sustained rise of 5+ bpm often means fatigue, illness, poor sleep or overtraining. A slow fall over months = improving fitness.

Weekly training load (TRIMP) – 12 weeks

Increase weekly load gradually (roughly ≤ 10–15 % per week).

Heart rate recovery (60 s)

Drop in bpm in the first minute after stopping. > 12 normal, > 25 very good. Higher over time = better fitness.

Orthostatic rise (standing − lying HR)

Compare with your own usual values; a rise noticeably above your normal can mean fatigue.

Aerobic threshold estimate (DFA α1 = 0.75)

Estimated from workouts that ramped gradually from easy to hard. Use it to set the top of your easy / zone 2 training.

Profile (used for zones, load and calories)

Zones: Z1 < 60 %, Z2 60–70 %, Z3 70–80 %, Z4 80–90 %, Z5 > 90 % of max HR. Max HR from a formula can be off by ±10 bpm – if you've seen a higher value in a hard effort, use that.

Not a medical device. A chest strap measures beat timing, not an ECG, and cannot diagnose heart rhythm problems. "Flagged beats" can be strap artefacts (dry electrodes, movement) or extra beats. If you see many flagged beats repeatedly at rest, or have palpitations, dizziness, chest pain or shortness of breath, see a doctor.
